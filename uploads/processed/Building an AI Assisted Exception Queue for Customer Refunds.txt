Building an AI Assisted Exception Queue for Customer Refunds
━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
What Happened

Refund requests often arrive through too many doors: support tickets, payment disputes, email, store systems, and account managers. A team can have a written policy and still lose time because every request must be read, interpreted, and routed manually.

An AI assisted exception queue gives operations a more orderly starting point. It can collect the request, identify the order and customer, summarize the reason, check basic policy signals, and place the case in a queue with a recommended path. Straightforward requests still follow rules. Unclear, high value, repeat, or policy-sensitive cases are clearly marked for a person.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Why It Matters for Businesses

The cost of a refund process is not limited to the money returned. Slow decisions create extra contacts, inconsistent treatment, and strained support teams. A consistent queue also makes it easier to see where returns, delivery issues, or product defects are creating demand.

Useful outcomes include:

• Faster first responses without automatic approval of risky cases
• A documented reason for each decision and escalation
• Fewer handoffs between support, finance, and fulfillment
• Better reporting on the causes behind refund volume

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
The Practical Automation Opportunity

Start with one intake path and a short, approved set of policy rules. Connect the workflow to order data, payment status, shipment tracking, and customer history. Let AI classify the request and draft a summary, but require human approval for cases above a set dollar amount, outside the return window, or involving a complaint trend.

Measure time to first action, percentage of cases resolved on the first pass, escalation rate, and repeat contacts. Review a sample of recommendations every week to find policy gaps and improve the routing logic.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━
Business Takeaway

Refund automation works best when it organizes judgment instead of pretending judgment is unnecessary. A well-designed exception queue gives customers quicker answers while keeping the decisions that affect trust in human hands.
